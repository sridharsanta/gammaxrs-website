The room

Wide enough to hold a master. Curved enough to move.

The main wall runs sixty feet across and twenty high on a 2.5 degree arc from centre to edge. That width holds a wide master without the frame finding the end of the world, and that curvature keeps parallax reading correctly as the camera travels.

An 18 by 15 foot ceiling panel carries the sky and the top light — the reason interiors and car roofs sit correctly rather than looking lit from the front. Three 3 by 6.5 foot panels each side move to wherever a reflection has to come from.

Camera tracking is Stype RedSpy, so the perspective on the wall is driven by where the lens actually is. The control desk sits on the floor with the crew, not in another building.

Environments

What goes on the wall.

Three routes, and the right one depends on how much the frame has to move rather than how much it has to look like.

Built for the script

A real-time environment our artists build with your production designer. Full parallax, full camera movement, lit to match your key.

Adapted from your reference

You arrive with a location scout, a matte or a stills library. We rebuild it as a plate that holds up to the moves you actually need.

Plate playback

Captured footage — driving plates, skies, crowds — played back in sync. The cheapest route when the camera is locked or nearly so.

How a shoot runs

Four stages, six weeks or six days.

  1. Scout and test

    You come to the floor with your DP. We put your reference on the wall and shoot a test on your lenses, so the decision is made on footage rather than a deck.

  2. Build

    Our artists build or adapt the environment with your production designer. You approve it running on the wall, at size, before the shoot day.

  3. Tech and pre-light

    A day on the floor with your gaffer to set the wall against your practical lighting and lock the colour pipeline.

  4. Shoot

    Our crew runs the wall alongside your unit. You leave with the shot, not with a plate and a note.

Honest limits

What this technique will not do.

01
Hard sunlight1000 nits is bright for a wall and dim for the sun. Sun keys come from a practical fixture; the wall does everything behind.
02
Frames that leave the wallSixty feet is wide, not infinite. A pan that finds the end of the volume is a location day.
03
Water and heavy atmosRain bars and standing water in front of an LED wall is a conversation about panel protection first and a shot second.
04
Very long lenses on a wide platePast a point the wall's pitch starts to show. We will tell you which lenses are safe on the test day.

Before you book.

How far out should we book?

Environment build is the long pole, not the stage. A bespoke environment wants four to six weeks from approved reference. A plate playback day can turn around in a week. [Current lead time — to confirm]

Can we shoot a test before committing?

Yes, and we would rather you did. Bring your camera and two lenses, we put your reference on the wall, and you leave with footage. It is the only honest way to decide.

Before you book.

Can I bring my own camera?

Yes, and most people do. A phone works for social formats; a cinema camera gets more out of the wall. Either way the operator will set the wall to your shutter and frame rate.

How many environments in a day?

As many as your shot list has, if they are already built. Switching is a playback change, not a set change.

Can two people be on camera?

Comfortably. Past three the room starts to dictate your blocking, and that is the point where the Signature Stage is worth the difference.

The premise

The audience is not the hard part any more. The production is.

Creators who have spent years learning what their audience watches hit the same wall moving up a format: the idea needs a crew, a stage and a pipeline, and those are priced for studios. So the work stays small, or it gets made badly once and abandoned.

We would rather spend stage downtime on those projects than on nothing. This is not sponsorship and it is not a competition — it is a working relationship with a small number of people each year.

What we look for

Three things, in this order.

  • An audience you built yourself, at any size, that returns
  • An idea that is one size too big for you right now
  • A reason it needs a volume rather than a location
  • Somebody on your side who can run a shoot day
What we do not take

So you do not waste an application.

  • Work that would be cheaper and better shot on a location
  • Brand campaigns with a client already attached — that is a booking
  • Projects where the stage is the idea rather than the setting
  • Anything that needs us to take the channel or the IP
How it works

From application to a finished pilot.

  1. Apply

    Send the channel, the idea, and what is stopping you making it. Three paragraphs is plenty. We read everything and we reply either way.

  2. Development session

    A working session on the floor with a producer and a technical director, to find out whether the idea and the technique actually suit each other. Some do not, and we will say so on the day.

  3. Shoot the pilot

    We allocate stage time and crew. You direct. The work stays yours, and so does the channel it goes out on.

  4. Take it to market

    Where it fits, we introduce it to partners who commission this kind of work — as co-producers, not as your vendor.
